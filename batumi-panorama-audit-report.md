# Batumi Panorama / Panorama Batumi (Charnali/Cernali) — public-facing audit

Date: 2026-05-29
Scope: public relations, marketing, sales presentation, online presence, buyer/investor experience
Method: public web search + extraction only

## Executive summary

This project presents online as a **small, low-rise, view-driven resort/investment complex in Charnali** near Batumi, but its public footprint is **fragmented, stale, contradictory, and credibility-damaging**.

The main issue is not just weak marketing. It is **trust collapse through inconsistent narratives**:
- some sources present it as an active off-plan project;
- some imply it was completed years ago;
- some resale listings suggest unfinished inventory is still circulating;
- the strongest independent construction-tracking source appears stale, with latest update around **February 2021**;
- official/public social footprint looks weak and not actively maintained;
- claims around ROI, completion, management, guarantees, and infrastructure are strong, but public proof is weak.

If your premise is that the project is "almost abandoned" and in difficulty, the public web does **not fully prove abandonment**, but it does strongly support this conclusion: **for an outside buyer/investor, the project feels under-maintained, under-communicated, and risky**.

## What the sources say

### Consistent claims across promotional sources
Across Korter / GEOLN / HomesOverseas / old social snippets, the repeated message is:
- location: **Charnali**, elevated hillside / ~300m above sea level;
- access: **10–15 min to Batumi**, **5 min to sea**, near airport/border;
- format: **low-rise resort/aparthotel** + cottages/townhouses;
- amenities promised: **spa, pool, restaurant, shop, security, concierge, playground**;
- sales promise: **installments, loans, bank backing, fast payback**;
- investor angle: **4–5 year payback**, sometimes **22% annual ROI**;
- emotional angle: peace, ecology, views, escape from dense Batumi.

### Key contradictions / red flags
1. **Construction status ambiguity**
   - Korter says latest construction update is **February 2021**.
   - GEOLN project page says **completion December 2019**.
   - HomesOverseas says **complex delivered**.
   - Resale listings in 2026 still market units in **white frame** condition with “planned infrastructure”.

2. **Unit-count inconsistency**
   - one source says **300 apartments**;
   - another says **100 units**;
   - another says 100 apartments + cottages/townhouses.
   This is a basic product-definition failure.

3. **Finishing inconsistency**
   - some sources say **turnkey**;
   - others say **white frame**;
   - GEOLN project page says **black walls**.
   That is a severe credibility problem for remote investors.

4. **Location framing inconsistency**
   - sometimes “10 minutes from Batumi,” sometimes 15;
   - “5 minutes from sea” but actual hilltop/separated geography matters;
   - promotional copy oversells proximity while underexplaining access friction.

5. **ROI / guarantee claims are aggressive**
   - 4-year payback;
   - 22% annual ROI;
   - management-company load assurances;
   - bank security / resell commitment.
   These are strong claims, but public evidence is weak and fragmented.

## Step 2 — report: situation and experience

## A. Public relations audit

### What is weak
- **No strong, current, authoritative public narrative.**
- The project does not dominate its own search results with fresh official content.
- Third-party portals carry most of the story.
- Social presence appears thin / stale / low-engagement.
- There is no obvious crisis-handling narrative addressing delays, completion state, operations, occupancy, resale, or management reality.

### PR effect
A project in difficulty needs **over-communication**. This one seems to have the opposite problem: **silence**.

Silence in real estate gets interpreted as:
- stalled construction,
- weak developer liquidity,
- low sales velocity,
- owner disputes,
- unfinished infrastructure,
- or failed rental promises.

Even if some of that is untrue, the public web currently lets buyers infer the worst.

## B. Marketing audit

### Positioning strength
The core positioning is actually coherent:
- low-rise,
- panoramic view,
- quieter alternative to dense Batumi towers,
- semi-resort lifestyle,
- investment + lifestyle hybrid.

That is not a bad concept.

### Marketing failure
The concept is not the problem. The problem is **proof**.

The marketing stack appears weak on:
- current photos and verified progress;
- live occupancy / actual use proof;
- real resident or owner testimonials;
- real operator / management proof;
- documented amenities actually working now;
- transparent inventory map;
- transparent legal and completion status;
- current website SEO dominance.

### Resulting buyer perception
The project reads like a **2019–2021 promise that never fully matured into a 2026 confidence story**.

## C. Sales audit

### Sales messaging observed
- installment plans;
- fast payback;
- low entry ticket;
- mortgage / bank comfort;
- turnkey promise;
- hotel/rental management promise.

### Sales credibility problems
For a suspicious buyer, these messages are too promotional relative to public proof.

Questions a buyer likely asks after 10 minutes online:
- Is it finished or not?
- How many units actually exist?
- Is anything operating there now?
- Who manages it?
- What is occupied vs empty?
- Which promised amenities were actually delivered?
- Why are third-party descriptions inconsistent?
- Why is the construction tracker stale?
- If it is good, why is the public footprint so weak?

If the sales team cannot answer these instantly with documents, videos, and live proof, conversion will collapse.

## D. Buyer / investor experience online

### Likely journey
1. Search project name.
2. Find aggregator pages and old portal descriptions.
3. Notice repetition of the same sales copy across sites.
4. Fail to find a strong, current, official truth source.
5. See resale/unfinished-unit evidence.
6. See stale progress tracking.
7. Conclude there is execution risk.

### Emotional experience
The buyer experience is:
- intrigued at first,
- then confused,
- then cautious,
- then suspicious,
- then likely lost to a cleaner competitor.

### Why this is especially damaging in Batumi/Adjara market
In this market, buyers already expect:
- overpromising,
- construction delays,
- uneven management quality,
- aggressive ROI pitches.

So if your public presence is not exceptionally clear, you get bucketed into the risky pile by default.

## E. Signals supporting “distressed / almost abandoned” perception
These are the strongest public signals that create that perception:

1. **Stale construction evidence**  
   Independent tracking appears old.

2. **Inconsistent status claims**  
   Off-plan / completed / unfinished all coexist.

3. **Dependence on aggregators**  
   The project is described mostly by portals, not by a strong official media machine.

4. **Secondary-market leakage**  
   Villas / units appear in resale channels in ways that feel like liquidation or owner exit rather than healthy ecosystem marketing.

5. **Low proof of active operations**  
   Weak evidence of vibrant, ongoing life at the site.

6. **Heavy promise language**  
   Strong ROI + management + bank-assurance copy without equally strong present-day proof.

## F. Signals that prevent a hard public conclusion of “abandoned”
To stay accurate: the public web does **not** let me verify full abandonment.

Counter-signals:
- resale inventory exists, implying the asset is at least partly real and marketable;
- some sources describe the complex as delivered/completed;
- some hospitality-like references imply some units may be usable or occupied;
- the product still circulates through property portals.

So the safe conclusion is:
**publicly, it looks compromised and weakly managed, not cleanly dead.**

## H. The core diagnosis in one line
**Batumi Panorama is being sold with the language of a polished investment product, but discovered online like a half-finished memory.**

## What they need to fix urgently

### Tier 1 — trust reconstruction
1. Publish one **official source of truth**.
2. State clearly: legal status, completion status, occupancy status, amenity status, inventory status.
3. Publish dated photo/video evidence from the site.
4. Publish a simple “what was promised / what exists today” matrix.
5. Remove or update contradictory third-party claims.

### Tier 2 — sales repair
6. Stop leading with ROI fantasy numbers unless backed by audited examples.
7. Replace generic investment copy with:
   - actual use cases,
   - actual owner stories,
   - actual yield cases,
   - actual transport/access explanation.
8. Build resale + primary inventory transparency.

### Tier 3 — PR repair
9. Address the elephant in the room directly: delays, partial completion, or relaunch, whichever is true.
10. Create a relaunch narrative: “why now, what changed, what is operational, what is next.”

## Suggested meeting posture for you

If you are meeting the developer, the sharpest line of questioning is:
- What is the **true current status** of the project?
- How many units are actually completed, sold, occupied, rentable?
- Which amenities are physically operational today?
- Why do public sources conflict on units, finish, and completion?
- Why is independent construction tracking stale?
- Who owns the customer relationship online?
- What is the current buyer funnel and where does it break?
- Is this a completion problem, sales problem, trust problem, or capital problem?

My view: it is primarily a **trust and narrative collapse layered on top of probable execution difficulty**.
