Core diagnosis
Batumi Panorama is being sold with the language of a polished investment product, but discovered like a half-finished memory.
Root causes

The problem is structural, not cosmetic.

1

No single source of truth

A buyer cannot confirm current status from one official place. The story is scattered across portals, listings, and old promotional fragments.

2

Proof is weaker than the promise

Investment claims are strong. Present-day evidence of completion, operation, occupancy, and delivery is weak.

3

Contradictions kill trust fast

Completed versus unfinished. Turnkey versus white frame. Different unit counts. The buyer stops believing before the sales team gets a chance to explain.

4

Silence is being read as distress

In a real estate market that already expects delays and overpromising, silence is interpreted as weakness, dispute, or failure.

What ownership needs to hear directly.

The project does not own its own first impression.

Search results are dominated by third-party distribution, not by one current official channel.

The online record creates doubt before contact.

A buyer sees stale progress, conflicting descriptions, and thin proof of active life.

The funnel breaks at trust, not at interest.

The concept can attract. The verification stage is where the buyer exits.

Aggressive investment language is under-supported.

Strong ROI and payback claims are not matched by equally strong present-day public proof.

Resale leakage weakens central sales control.

When the product appears in secondary channels with mixed framing, the market reads it as stress.

Silence is doing active damage.

Without a clear explanation, the market fills the gap with the worst possible assumptions.

How to solve it

The fix is a trust reconstruction program.

1. Publish one official source of truth

One landing page. One current story. Dated photos, dated video, legal status, completion status, occupancy status, amenity status, inventory status.

2. Replace promise-first messaging with proof-first messaging

Lead with what exists now. Stop asking the buyer to imagine. Show the building, the life, the management, the access, and the actual use cases.

3. Eliminate public contradictions

Correct third-party descriptions. Align unit counts, finish levels, and completion claims. Remove any claim that cannot be defended in a meeting.

4. Build a relaunch narrative

If the project went through delay, partial completion, or restructuring, say it clearly. Then explain what changed, what is operational now, and what happens next.

5. Show human life, not just product copy

Use residents, owners, operating spaces, and real site moments. The project needs to feel inhabited, managed, and alive.

6. Rebuild the sales funnel around trust checkpoints

Every buyer question should have an immediate answer: what is finished, what is available, who manages it, what is operating, and why this project is safe to believe now.
